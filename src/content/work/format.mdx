<Section title="The Brief Behind the Brief">
<Column>

My first brief was to design the missing pages: records, insights, companies and people, so every name in a report led somewhere. Over the next five months that grew to more than 15 pages, built with **Martin Dreymann**, Format’s co-founder and CTO.

</Column>
<Column>

But the pages weren’t the whole problem. Customers were already asking their own questions of Format through Claude, and when an answer came back there was no way to tell whether it was right.

</Column>
</Section>

<Crop window={[1416, 812]} bg="var(--neutral-050)" caption="A company page">

<Chapter label="Asking twice" glow>

<Section title="Same Question, Different Answers">
<Column>

So I asked the product what customers ask it, using Format’s own integration, the same question two ways. A grouped count on one archived topic found 59 named companies. The live answer said 4, and marked the theme dormant, last seen in January. A customer had asked about it that July.

</Column>
<Column>

**Outcome:**

It wasn’t the model. The 329 insights behind the 59 sat under a topic that had been archived, and only 28% of the corpus sat under the eight topics still being counted. The wrong number mattered less than the fact that nobody could tell it was wrong.

</Column>
</Section>

<Section title="Show Your Working">
<Column>

That’s what the MCP reports were built around. A report shows its sources as counts of records, insights and themes, every claim clicks through to the conversation behind it, and the insights it’s connected to sit underneath.

</Column>
<Column>

Reports are also shared as links. A teammate opens one and pulls it straight into Claude or ChatGPT with one button, so the product shows what it can do before anyone has set anything up.

</Column>
</Section>

<Section title="Bring Your Own AI">
<Column>

The MCP and skills page is where that starts. Martin shipped a first version quietly on 10 June, and I designed the one that launched in the week of 23 June: skills first, each a ready-made workflow like finding case-study candidates or briefing an account, with the setup for each AI tool a click away.

</Column>
<Column>

**Outcome:**

75% of Format’s users connected it to an AI tool. Before launch, my design QA of the build found 31 issues on that one page, from a dialog missing its Close and Finish buttons to tabs a screen reader couldn’t use.

</Column>
</Section>

<Chapter label="The system" glow>

<Section title="A System That Checks Itself">
<Column>

When I arrived, the codebase had a set of colour tokens nothing used, colours that didn’t belong together, and about 65 stock shadcn components. I replaced them with five OKLCH hue ramps, an eleven-step neutral with a cool tint, and semantic names the components actually use.

</Column>
<Column>

Most of Format’s code was written by agents, mine included, so the system needed rules they’d read. I wrote three.

</Column>
</Section>

<Section title="Fifty Shades of Grey">
<Column>

The rules came from a mistake of mine. In April I wired Tailwind’s grey classes to the branded neutrals, and the wiring silently lost to Tailwind’s own defaults. For two months the app showed generic greys, and it looked fine, because the code Figma generates carries hex fallbacks that hid it.

</Column>
<Column>

**Change:**

My design QA of the platform in June caught it, along with type sizes and buttons that had drifted from the designs. Martin fixed the wiring, and I re-synced the tokens and 14 components against Figma. Since then the rule is to check what renders in the browser, not what the CSS says.

</Column>
</Section>

</Chapter>

<Chapter label="Spotlight" glow>

<Section title="The Trigger, Not the Card">
<Column>

Spotlight was a hover card for the people and companies named in a report. Design was due on a Tuesday, engineering on Wednesday and launch on Thursday, the week of 10 August. The card was the brief.

</Column>
<Column>

But cards only appear when a report mentions someone, and the mentions were only generated some of the time. A well-designed card in a report with no mentions ships to nobody. I flagged it, named the two fixes as engineering’s, and spent the design time on what the card had to survive.

</Column>
</Section>

<Section title="Partial Is Normal">
<Column>

No data yet, partial data, below the confidence threshold, a person we couldn’t match, someone who’d left, a fetch that failed. Partial was the most likely state, so it had to look designed, not broken.

</Column>
<Column>

I also moved mobile into scope, because people read reports on their phones. And I raised a question nobody had asked: reports get shared outside the company, so a card showing CRM details on hover is internal data leaving the building.

</Column>
</Section>

</Chapter>

<Chapter label="What happened next" glow>

<Section title="Insight World">
<Column>

On the side, I built a 3D prototype in Claude Code to ask how you’d see what an AI answer was built from. Quotes cluster into themes, themes into trends, and clicking one lights up the evidence behind it while everything else dims.

</Column>
<Column>

It never became a feature, and it wasn’t meant to. It was a way to start a conversation about trust, and the team kept using it to look at their own data. The data here is invented, so you can [explore it live](/insight-world/).

</Column>
</Section>

<Section title="Looking Back">
<Column>

I wrapped up in August 2026. The record and insight pages went from no usage to a third of all usage between them, with reports still the most visited. Customers weren’t just reading the report any more. They were checking what was behind it.

</Column>
<Column>

**What’s next:**

We only counted those numbers afterwards, so next time I’d agree the two or three a change should move before designing it. And I’d settle what correcting the AI means: an edit that sticks, a fix to the input, or delete and restate. Format sat closest to the second, while customers expected the first.

</Column>
</Section>
