<Section title="The Brief Behind the Brief">
<Column>

My first brief was to design the missing pages: records, insights, companies and people, so every name in a report led somewhere. Over five months at two days a week, that grew to **more than 15 pages**, built with **Martin Dreymann**, Format’s co-founder and CTO.

</Column>
<Column>

The bigger problem was trust. Customers had started asking Format questions through Claude, and when an answer came back, nothing showed whether it was right.

</Column>

<Section title="Bring Your Own AI">
<Column>

Martin shipped a quiet first version of Format’s MCP page on 10 June, built around the setup steps. I redesigned it around what you’d use it for: skills, each a ready-made workflow like finding case-study candidates or briefing an account, with the setup for each tool a click away. It launched the week of 23 June.

</Column>
<Column>

**Outcome:**

**75% of Format’s users** connected it to an AI tool. Before launch I QA’d the build and logged **31 issues** on that one page, from a dialog with no way to close it to tabs a screen reader couldn’t reach.

</Column>
</Section>

<Chapter label="Asking twice" glow>

<Section title="Same Question, Different Answers">
<Column>

In July, before we decided what Format’s reports should say, I wanted to know what customers were already asking it. So I used the product to find out, through Claude, and asked it two ways which customers were stitching their own tools together to get answers from their data. Format’s own summary said **4**, and called the theme dormant since January. Pulling the raw insights and grouping them by company found **59**, and a customer had raised it that month.

</Column>
<Column>

The model wasn’t the problem. Those insights were filed under a topic that had since been archived, and Format’s answers only counted active topics, which held **28%** of everything it had heard. The wrong number was bad. That nobody could tell it was wrong was worse.

</Column>
</Section>

</Column>

Reports are shared as links, too. Whoever opens one can take it into Claude or ChatGPT with one button and keep asking, so the product shows what it can do before anyone has set it up.

<Chapter label="The system" glow>

<Section title="One Name, Two Places">
<Column>

When I arrived, the codebase had a set of colour tokens nothing used, colours that didn’t belong together, and about 65 stock shadcn components. I replaced them with five OKLCH hue ramps and an eleven-step neutral with a cool tint, in two layers: primitives for the raw values, and semantic names the components actually use.

</Column>
<Column>

Figma holds the same two layers as variables, so a colour has one name in both places. The rule between them is short: code owns structure and behaviour, Figma owns visual values, so an argument is settled before it starts.

</Column>
</Section>

<FormatVariables />

<Section title="A System That Checks Itself">
<Column>

Format’s team, me included, wrote most of the code with AI agents. They’re quick and confident, and they’ll tell you a change worked when it didn’t. So I wrote the loop every component went through: read the rules and every past mistake, take the values from Figma rather than a screenshot, build with tokens only, then check what actually renders in the browser against the design.

</Column>
<Column>

An agent got three tries to match the design before the component came back to me, and nothing shipped without my sign-off. The last step writes down what went wrong. When the Button came back with 8px of padding instead of 12, that became a rule every later build read first.

</Column>
</Section>

<Chapter label="Spotlight" glow>

<Section title="The Trigger, Not the Card">
<Column>

Spotlight is the card behind every name in a report. A mention carries the company’s logo after the person’s name, so one chip says who and where, and hovering it opens the card with the rest. Design was due on a Tuesday, engineering on Wednesday and launch on Thursday, the week of 10 August.

</Column>
<Column>

The card was the brief. But cards only appear where a report mentions someone, and mentions were only generated some of the time. A well-designed card in a report with no mentions ships to nobody. I flagged it to engineering as theirs to fix, and spent the design time on what the card had to survive.

</Column>
</Section>

<Section title="Partial Is Normal">
<Column>

Most cards wouldn’t be complete. There might be no data yet, only some of it, data below the confidence threshold, a person we couldn’t match, someone who’d left or a fetch that failed. Partial was the most likely state, so it had to look designed, not broken. I designed those states in code, straight into the component, rather than in Figma.

</Column>
<Column>

I also moved mobile into scope, because people read reports on their phones. And I raised a question nobody had asked: reports get shared outside the company, so a card showing CRM details on hover is internal data leaving the building.

</Column>
</Section>

<Section title="Insight World">
<Column>

On the side, I built a 3D prototype in Claude Code to work out how you might see what an AI answer is built from. Quotes cluster into themes, themes into trends, and clicking one lights up the evidence behind it while everything else dims.

</Column>
<Column>

It never became a feature, and it wasn’t meant to. It was a way to start a conversation about trust, and the team used it to see how their own data connected. The data here is invented, so it’s yours to explore below.

</Column>
</Section>

<Section title="Moving Forward">
<Column>

By the time I wrapped up in August 2026, the record and insight pages had gone from no usage to **a third of all usage** between them, with reports still the most visited. Customers weren’t just reading reports any more. They were checking what was behind them, and that’s the direction Format should keep pushing.

</Column>
<Column>

**What’s next:**

Two things decide where it goes from here. The first is measurement: agree the two or three numbers a change should move before designing it, rather than counting afterwards as we did. The second is correction. Customers expect an edit to an AI answer to stick, while Format was built to fix the input and let the answer change. Settling that is what turns reports people can check into reports they’ll act on.

</Column>
</Section>
