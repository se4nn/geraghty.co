<Section title="The Brief Behind the Brief">
<Column>

My first brief was to design the missing pages: records, insights, companies and people, so every name in a report led somewhere. Over the next five months that grew to more than 15 pages, built with **Martin Dreymann**, Format’s co-founder and CTO.

</Column>
<Column>

But the pages weren’t the whole problem. Customers were already asking their own questions of Format through Claude, and when an answer came back there was no way to tell whether it was right.

</Column>

<Chapter label="Asking twice" glow>

<Section title="Same Question, Different Answers">
<Column>

In July I wanted to know what customers were asking Claude before they asked Format, so I asked Format, through its own Claude integration, in two ways. Counting the insights directly found 59 companies talking about it. Format’s own answer said 4, and called the theme dormant since January. A customer had raised it that same month.

</Column>
<Column>

**Outcome:**

The model wasn’t wrong. The 329 insights behind the 59 sat under a topic that had been archived, and only 28% of everything Format had heard sat under the topics it still counted. A wrong number was bad. Nobody being able to tell it was wrong was worse.

</Column>
</Section>

<Section title="Show Your Working">
<Column>

So the reports Claude writes through Format were built to show their working. Each one counts its sources (records, insights and themes), every claim clicks through to the conversation behind it, and the insights it draws on sit underneath.

</Column>
<Column>

Reports are also shared as links. A teammate opens one and pulls it straight into Claude or ChatGPT with one button, so the product shows what it can do before anyone has set anything up.

</Column>

<Section title="Bring Your Own AI">
<Column>

The MCP and skills page is where that starts. Martin shipped a first version quietly on 10 June, and I designed the one that launched in the week of 23 June: skills first, each a ready-made workflow like finding case-study candidates or briefing an account, with the setup for each AI tool a click away.

</Column>
<Column>

**Outcome:**

75% of Format’s users connected it to an AI tool. Before launch, my design QA of the build found 31 issues on that one page, from a dialog missing its Close and Finish buttons to tabs a screen reader couldn’t use.

</Column>
</Section>

<Section title="A System That Checks Itself">
<Column>

When I arrived, the codebase had a set of colour tokens nothing used, colours that didn’t belong together, and about 65 stock shadcn components. I replaced them with five OKLCH hue ramps and an eleven-step neutral with a cool tint, in two layers: primitives for the raw values, and semantic names the components actually use.

</Column>
<Column>

Figma holds the same two layers as variables, so a colour has one name in both places. The rule between them is short: code owns structure and behaviour, Figma owns visual values, so an argument is settled before it starts.

</Column>
</Section>

<FormatVariables />

<Section title="Seven Steps, Every Time">
<Column>

Format’s team, me included, wrote most of its code with AI agents. They’re quick and confident, and they’ll tell you a change worked when it didn’t. So I wrote the loop every component went through: read the rules and every past mistake, take the values from Figma rather than a screenshot, build with tokens only, then check what actually renders in the browser against the design.

</Column>
<Column>

An agent got three tries to match the design, then it came back to me, and nothing shipped without my sign-off. The last step writes down what went wrong. When the Button came back with 8px of padding instead of 12, that became a rule every later build read first.

</Column>
</Section>

<Section title="The Trigger, Not the Card">
<Column>

Spotlight was a hover card for the people and companies named in a report. Design was due on a Tuesday, engineering on Wednesday and launch on Thursday, the week of 10 August. The card was the brief.

</Column>
<Column>

But cards only appear when a report mentions someone, and the mentions were only generated some of the time. A well-designed card in a report with no mentions ships to nobody. I flagged it to engineering as theirs to fix, and spent the design time on what the card had to survive.

</Column>
</Section>

<Section title="Partial Is Normal">
<Column>

No data yet, partial data, below the confidence threshold, a person we couldn’t match, someone who’d left, a fetch that failed. Partial was the most likely state, so it had to look designed, not broken. I designed those states in code, straight into the component, rather than in Figma.

</Column>
<Column>

I also moved mobile into scope, because people read reports on their phones. And I raised a question nobody had asked: reports get shared outside the company, so a card showing CRM details on hover is internal data leaving the building.

</Column>
</Section>

<Chapter label="What happened next" glow>

<Section title="Insight World">
<Column>

On the side, I built a 3D prototype in Claude Code to ask how you’d see what an AI answer was built from. Quotes cluster into themes, themes into trends, and clicking one lights up the evidence behind it while everything else dims.

</Column>
<Column>

It never became a feature, and it wasn’t meant to. It was a way to start a conversation about trust, and the team used it to explore and visualise how their own data connected. The data in it is invented, so it’s open to explore below.

</Column>
</Section>

<Section title="Looking Back">
<Column>

I wrapped up in August 2026. The record and insight pages went from no usage to a third of all usage between them, with reports still the most visited. Customers weren’t just reading the report any more. They were checking what was behind it.

</Column>
<Column>

**What’s next:**

We only counted those numbers afterwards, so next time I’d agree the two or three a change should move before designing it. And I’d settle what correcting the AI means: an edit that sticks, a fix to the input, or delete and restate. Format sat closest to the second, while customers expected the first.

</Column>
</Section>
